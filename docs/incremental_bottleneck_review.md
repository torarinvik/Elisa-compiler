# Incremental compilation bottleneck review

Reviewed 2026-10-19 from the local build notes, retained-edit benchmark, compiler LLVM dump, and the Go compiler's ARM64 disassembly.

## What the measurements show

The stage1 build profile in [build-speed-notes.md](build-speed-notes.md) is historical (base `2678ff10`), not a profile of the current working tree. It attributed 46% of samples to semantic analysis, 39% to LLVM IR generation, and 4% to O0 object emission. `check_region_storage_stability` accounted for 17.6% of samples; its `param_growth_rows` and `pgs_callee_fields` work accounted for 9.6% and 7.6%. The current `param_growth_summary.elisa` already has per-round dependency scheduling and indexed callee rows, so that old profile does not justify another isolated lookup tweak. A fresh matching stage1 profile is needed before ranking today's self-hosted hotspots.

Historical default-build measurements in [stage1_performance.md](stage1_performance.md) show a different priority from the O0 object-cache experiments: whole-module LLVM O2 took 92.7 seconds of a 134-second self-host build, and a later round measured 97 seconds of 143 seconds. The associated pass profile covered 2.03 million IR instructions and 9,350 functions; InstCombine was about 20% of pass time, followed by the Inliner at 7.6%. A parallel ThinLTO prototype reduced optimizer-plus-codegen wall time on a loaded host, but the resulting compiler failed runtime parity and was not adopted. These profiles are old Linux snapshots, not current-source measurements. They show why an O0 shard cache alone cannot address the historical default O2 bottleneck, and why O2 reuse needs an optimization boundary that preserves cross-function imports and passes runtime parity.

The available `build/s1_driver.ll` is not usable for machine-code conclusions. Its `Semantic.check_region_storage_stability` wrapper only loads file fields and calls the declaration walker, but Clang rejects the module elsewhere: `%__packed_payload_108` receives an `i64` for an `i32` field at IR line 213991. Consequently the requested `-O0` assembly could not be generated from this dump. Do not use this stale dump as evidence about current generated code.

The saved `build/verification/current-seed-bb0b884b/` attempt is also not a current compiler result. Its 1,186-file input manifest was unchanged during the run, but Stage1 seeding stopped at the default 6 GiB RSS guard (`rc=125`, peak 6,443,352,064 bytes) after lexing in 565 ms and parsing in 2.204 s; it never reached semantic analysis or IR emission. Comparing that manifest with today's tree finds 630 changed inputs. Raising `ELISA_STAGE1_SEED_MAX_RSS_KB` may allow a later stable run to reach actionable diagnostics, but this artifact supplies none.

The source-frozen semantic profile under `build/verification/frozen-seed-profile-bb0b884b/` is useful for allocation leads, but not as a current-source result: it pins the older Core revision `bb0b884b`, exits during semantic analysis before codegen, and reports 223.6 s elapsed. Its CPU profile is dominated by Go heap clearing (`runtime.memclrNoHeapPointers`, 39.5% flat), map assignment (15.1% cumulative), and `madvise` (11.1% flat). Its allocation profile attributes 3.32 GB to `cloneTrackedValueTypeWithSeenDepth`, 2.05 GB to `strings.Builder.WriteString`, 1.30 GB to `strings.FieldsFunc`, 1.15 GB to `abstractParamRegionRefState`, and 6.29 GB cumulatively to `smtCheckVCMulti`. These identify semantic allocation and GC pressure worth re-profiling after a successful, source-matched seed; they do not rank today's LLVM or object-emission path.

A two-second macOS stack sample of the manifest-matched `2e03e4a9…` snapshot's Stage0 seed process (2026-10-19, `/private/tmp/elisa-stage1-snapshot-r30.sample.txt`) adds a preliminary semantic hotspot: `Analyzer.storeFlowStmt` appeared in 246 of 1,348 sampled main-thread stacks (about 18%), with repeated `storeScanBindingWrites` and `localContainerElementScan` frames also visible. A second two-second sample from the later, source-qualified `2d490a8c…` R32 seed (`/private/tmp/elisa-stage1-r32-20261019.sample.txt`) saw 1,386 main-thread samples; `localContainerElementScan.walkStructFields` and `walkValue` each appeared 105 times, `walkNode` 99 times, and the block/region-clone paths 104 times each, while `storeFlowStmt` appeared 27 times. The changing mix reinforces that these are phase snapshots, not stable rankings: stack counts overlap and vary with the code being analyzed. Both samples cover only the Stage0 compiler process, not its Z3 child, and end before LLVM codegen. Use them to prioritize a matched analyzer profile, not to rank whole-build cost.

R35 and R36 both ran against the source-matched `eaabf5e0…` / `76877fbd…` snapshot and failed in Stage0 semantic analysis before LLVM emission; they produced the same 164 distinct non-warning diagnostic location/message pairs with two different Core binaries. In R35, a two-second Stage0 sample at 00:46:15 had the compiler's main thread waiting in `pthread_cond_wait`; the paired Z3 sample at 00:46:18 placed all 1,768 sampled stacks in `parse_smt2_commands` through the SMT-LIB scanner and `getc`. A later R35 sample at 00:47:08 showed a semantic goroutine in `evaluateLateReturnBorrowFrames` / `computeReturnBorrowFlowForFunc` and recursive return-borrow statement flow; that sample reported an 11.7G physical footprint. In R36, the comparable sample at 00:50:52 showed prominent Go GC-worker stacks (`runtime.(*mspan).moveInlineMarks`: 506 samples; `spanInlineMarkBits.init`: 188; `mallocgcSmallScanHeader`: 72; sweep: 24) while the active semantic goroutine was in `validatePermissionUsage` and permission-statement walking; physical footprint was 13.3G. The source-matched samples therefore point to SMT input parsing, recursive return-borrow analysis, permission validation, and substantial allocation/GC work as leads; their short windows and changing stacks do not establish a stable whole-build ranking. ## Retained session memory and lifetime

The original session declarations had dynamic arrays and a dictionary without region parameters or per-field region annotations; qualifying only a function's `session` reference did not establish that nested storage survived request teardown. The current worktree now introduces `IncrementalSemanticSession[@r]` and a separate `IncrementalSemanticSessionSnapshot[@r]`, annotates retained collections `@r`, copies portable facts into that snapshot, and leaves invocation anchors in the request-owned snapshot. New controls now cover a generic struct with an empty `darray[...] @r` field that grows through the struct, reject a request-borrowed view stored in a session region, and drive a small-to-128-included-functions-to-same-large sequence through request teardown. The gate compiles the direct field control but does not execute its generated code; the live session sequence requires the repeated large request to hit with at least 100 eligible function bodies, all matched to retained facts. These controls have been reviewed statically but the gate has not yet been run, so the ownership change remains unvalidated by the compiler. The opt-in session remains a semantic-only host. The ordinary CLI now passes a fresh request-scoped session through its compile path, but discards it at request end and cannot reuse facts across separate invocations, so it is not yet an edit-to-executable speedup. Retain no token slices, AST-backed names, or other request views.

There is a separate request-peak issue: candidate scanning stages findings and then staged fact/anchor arrays, while edit replay also stages rebound anchors. A review estimate puts the peak near 176 bytes per finding before allocator growth when those buffers coexist. Reclaim candidate scratch in a nested region, or stream with rollback so a body is committed only after the full scan is known to be complete. These lifetime and peak-memory issues are independent: persistent fields need explicit ownership, and temporary buffers need bounded lifetimes.

The semantic query graph now has a private, session-owned key/evaluate/commit protocol with opaque generation-checked handles, exact byte comparisons, bounded dependency sets, and reverse invalidation. The session collector consults `SourceInput` and `FunctionBodyFacts` nodes on changed-source requests, and publishes their canonical byte projections after a successful snapshot store. A body query is attempted only after the trusted prior snapshot proves an exact declaration surface, cacheable prior record, and exact body match. A body hit restores canonical query bytes into the current request snapshot after validating them against the previous typed facts. If a graph lookup or restore misses, the collector still copies prior typed facts under the exact-surface and exact-body checks; it scans the current body only when that fallback is unavailable or invalid. The publisher still rebuilds and reevaluates the source query after collection. For body-query hits restored successfully, request-local markers let publication reacquire a fresh body-query handle and skip duplicate body copying, hashing, and evaluation only when graph generation, exact query key, body record span in the unchanged request snapshot, body result generation, SourceInput result generation, Green dependencies, and graph health still match. Markers retain request-local IDs and generation evidence, never query handles; graph clears and retries invalidate them. The `body_query_publish_skips` counter exposes this path. Source-value reconstruction and evaluation, plus all misses, remain on the existing path. Canonical direct-fact results are serialized and committed only on misses. This is an unmeasured work-reduction change, not a speedup claim. No source-matched retained-edit benchmark qualifies the graph path, and it has not passed parity controls. The parse and snapshot path remains authoritative.

`bodies_matched` and `body_facts_reused` remain aggregate counts. The collector now also reports `function_body_facts_restored`, `prior_snapshot_fact_fallbacks`, and `cold_body_scans`; `source_input_admitted` is true only when the source query hits and the exact trusted declaration surface also matches. The semantic-session control asserts both a body-fact graph restore and source-query admission on its inserted-line case. These counters make the route observable, but the control has not been run against a source-matched compiler, so they do not yet qualify correctness or speedup.

Per-value preflight guards are now present in the live source: the collector and publisher check the exact encoded `SourceInput` size before making its graph value copy, body lengths above 16 MiB skip graph evaluation, the span evaluator checks its range and length before comparison, and direct-fact result size is validated before serialization reserves or appends bytes. `FunctionBodyFacts` evaluation in both the collector and publisher now reads the body directly from the request snapshot span and reuses the record's FNV hash instead of allocating and re-hashing a temporary body buffer. A fingerprint match still requires exact byte comparison, which remains O(body length); on a publication miss, the publisher materializes `body_input` only when it needs to commit the query value. Candidate matching now checks body length and hash before comparing bytes; only equal-length, equal-hash bodies reach the exact current-versus-prior comparison. A restored body still pays for that exact comparison, then the graph evaluator compares it with the committed query input, so a hit can make two full byte walks over an unchanged body. Hashes only reject candidates and never authorize reuse. Remove that second walk only if a trusted generation marker proves the committed graph input is the exact prior snapshot body; the FNV fingerprint alone is insufficient. The typed snapshot remains the fallback for values rejected by these checks. The collector still assembles the proposed declaration surface needed for exact snapshot comparison. These per-value checks do not preflight aggregate graph admission: a permitted source input or canonical result, and a body input on a commit miss, can still be materialized before commit rejects it against the 32 MiB logical value pool. If a publication attempt is rejected, including by aggregate capacity, the publisher clears the graph and retries once from the current committed snapshot; if the retry also fails, it leaves the graph empty and the typed-snapshot fallback remains authoritative. The per-value checks bound temporary input/result work, but these temporaries are outside the aggregate budget, and logical payload accounting does not bound allocator capacity retained across edits. A remaining-capacity preflight or resettable region is still needed to bound retained memory. Keep the graph opt-in. These changes have source review only and still need parity and retained-session performance evidence.

Key admission returns a miss on a cap rejection without mutating the graph. Commit validates candidate handles, exact dependency identity, committed result generations, and cycles before replacing the edge set. Logical value/aggregate overflow clears the graph; the publisher then makes the single rebuild retry described above, leaving it empty if publication fails again. Malformed adjacency and worklist overflow poison it so affected work cannot be published Green. Stale dependers become `PotentiallyDirty` and can return Green only after their direct dependencies are Green and their observed result generations are current. Dependency deduplication still uses bounded nested scans, but current callers pass at most one dependency (zero for `SourceInput` and `NameLookup`, one `SourceInput` edge for `FunctionBodyFacts`), so this is a scalability concern rather than a measured hot path. Preflight already builds a unique ID list, making the apply-side duplicate scan redundant under its private caller contract. If future queries add per-name or per-symbol dependencies, measure fanout and the full check-and-commit path before adding a scratch set. `ifq_graph_within_limit` budgets logical counts for the key/value/node/edge pools, while `darray.reserve` can retain larger capacities and `ifq_clear_session_graph` clears/truncates without releasing those buffers. Persistent sessions keep graph evaluation/publication disconnected by default; the explicit `ELISA_STAGE1_INCREMENTAL_QUERY_GRAPH=1` opt-in publishes on cold starts and changed-source requests. The 32 MiB limit is a logical admission budget per graph generation; it does not include prior arena backing blocks, dictionary bucket tables, or pre-admission temporaries, so it is not a hard retained-session memory bound. Keep the graph opt-in until those allocations are charged across generations or it uses a resettable region. No parity or retained-edit performance comparison qualifies the graph path; misses and failed publication still use the exact typed-fact fallback where available and otherwise scan the current body.

The arena implementation confirms how retained capacity grows: `arena_da_reserve` and `arena_da_append_many` double capacity (`elisacore_std/collections.elisa:190-220,855-884`). `arena_realloc` extends a tail allocation in place when possible; only a non-tail move copies and retains the old backing until arena teardown (`elisacore_std/arena.elisa:1505-1523,1552-1567`). If each pool's capacity grows geometrically and never shrinks, retained array generations sum to less than twice its largest backing buffer; this is a conditional per-pool estimate, not yet a proven whole-graph bound. Truncating a pool must not subtract those bytes. Dictionary rehashing uses a current/spare stack ping-pong and returns the old table as spare, so count both tables rather than every historical generation (`elisacore_std/collections.elisa:1193-1216`). A hard retained-memory budget should charge actual growth allocations or use measured capacity, and should include any pre-admission temporaries separately.

The opt-in semantic-session host is a separate semantic-only protocol. An exact hit still rereads and expands includes, tokenizes and parses the source, clones and reanchors retained facts, resolves declarations, runs the parallel pass batches, and invokes the canonical whole-file checkers. The snapshot does let selected checkers skip narrower body work: Global-authority can skip bodies with no relevant direct facts or replay direct-call records, effect-source and ungranted-panic coverage can skip bodies proven to have no direct facts, and self-assignment checks can replay complete fact ranges. These are checker-specific walk reductions; semantic results are not cached, and the rest of the pipeline still runs. The private query graph restores canonical body-fact results into the current snapshot after checking them against the prior typed facts. It does not reuse parse trees or LLVM/native artifacts. Ordinary CLI invocations construct a fresh session and release it after each compile, while the host retains its session between checks. The host now has independent spans for request total, include expansion, lex/parse, static preprocessing with any generated-source retokenization and reparse, incremental fact collection, and semantic passes. Inner spans overlap the request-total span and must not be summed as disjoint stages. This instrumentation creates boundaries for later measurement but is not itself performance evidence.

Source review of `semantic_api.elisa` confirms that an exact session hit still enters `check_full_into_pipeline`: it reseeds builtin state, recollects symbols and metadata, rebuilds the loop view, and dispatches multiple `par_pass_run` batches. The snapshot gates selected subchecks but does not restore whole-phase semantic results. Measure full request time and pass work before ranking clone or sidecar micro-costs; professional-scale reuse needs typed syntax or phase-query results with dependency invalidation.

The semantic driver consumes the temporary diagnostic result inside a short region and keeps only the error count plus the diagnostics that the current reporter emits (warnings as well in interpret mode); the rebuilt `SymbolTable` does not escape this gate and is deliberately released before codegen. That makes a strict exact-request result memo a plausible no-op optimization only for the persistent `semantic-session` host in terminal `-emit check` mode: store a clean-result sentinel when no diagnostics exist, or retain/replay the complete reportable diagnostic stream. The one-shot CLI has no cross-invocation marker, and compile modes may depend on semantic state or checker side effects, so they need separate proof before any checker is skipped. Key the check-mode memo by exact root/include identity, semantic options, reporter mode, and engine version; otherwise rerun the pipeline. This is a source-level opportunity, not yet parity- or timing-qualified.

For a future whole-semantic-diagnostics no-op memo, `check_const_float_cast` is an option-key trap: it reads `ELISA_STAGE1_WASM` directly, while the encoded semantic option key does not include that environment setting. This does not affect current body-fact reuse because the target-sensitive checker remains in the full semantic pass and reruns. The current session protocols accept only check/compile and quit commands and replay fixed process arguments, so the effective target mode is stable for an epoch; include it in the memo key defensively or document and enforce that invariant. Keep the fast path at the fresh-diagnostics wrapper, require both current fresh diagnostics and the complete prior reported stream to be empty (including warnings and proof-hole hints), preserve `run_never_leak`, and account for `ELISA_STAGE1_TIMINGS` output and `ELISA_SEMANTIC_SERIAL` scheduling. The lower-level API also accepts caller-owned nonempty diagnostic tables.

Any broader query-level memo also needs an explicit side-effect policy. rustc represents diagnostics and used-feature updates as tracked query side effects and replays them when the owning node is green; see its [query-side-effect implementation](https://github.com/rust-lang/rust/blob/main/compiler/rustc_middle/src/dep_graph/graph.rs). Elisa's narrow clean-check memo can require an empty diagnostic stream; a memo that skips effectful semantic work must retain and replay the complete reportable effects and required per-request bookkeeping.

Persistent `semantic-session` and `compile-session` hosts keep query-graph evaluation and publication disabled unless `ELISA_STAGE1_INCREMENTAL_QUERY_GRAPH=1` is set. Requests with no eligible reads skip this pass; a request with at least one still pays O(import annotations + symbols + members + regions). The build is included in whole-request timing but omitted from the chain counter. Key schema 2 stores only current module and exact name, while identity/options mismatches reset the session graph. This source change still needs a source-matched build and parity validation. The ordinary `resolve_declarations` entry point uses a disabled context and stays on that cold path. The prime request is recorded separately and excluded from warm body-edit timing ratios. Graph-off visits more rows and pays more telemetry work, so current timing ratios may overstate graph-on gains; they are not production-equivalent until a counter-disabled timing arm exists. Short-lived sessions currently use one-element `darray`s so the borrowed session slot has a stable address under the compiler's lifetime checks. That adds an allocation for the one-shot CLI session and each standalone fact-collection or semantic-gate fallback call; the persistent compile-session host allocates its slot once per epoch. A separate valid arm64 probe, `build/verification/local-slice-native.ll`, does show how contract-heavy source can affect unoptimized code shape. Its user-written `string_view_slice` has three large `ensure` formulas and repeated assertions. The saved IR function body contains 326 branch terminators, 147 integer comparisons, 99 phi nodes, and 10 allocas. In the saved O0 executable, the target-width `view_len.i64() != view.len` check also survives as `cmp x8, x8; b.eq` in `_string_view_slice` at `0x100000478`/`0x10000047c`; IR lines 373-380 load the copied `view_len` and the same `view.len` field. This is a concrete redundant check in this arm64 code shape, not a measured runtime cost. Recompiling the saved IR with Homebrew LLVM 23.1.3 for `arm64-apple-darwin27.0.0` and `-mcpu=apple-m5`, then counting encoded instructions in the object, gives 1,589 instructions across 353 labeled blocks at `llc -O0`, with 698 `[sp]`-referencing instructions and a 1,152-byte local stack allocation plus a 16-byte callee-save pair. An O0 pass ablation (`opt -S -passes=...` followed by `llc -O0 -mtriple=arm64-apple-darwin27.0.0 -mcpu=apple-m5`) gives 1,571 instructions/680 stack references after `mem2reg`, 935/395 after `instcombine,simplifycfg`, and 770/266 after `mem2reg,instcombine,simplifycfg`; `mem2reg` alone barely changes code size, while combining arithmetic and simplifying the CFG remove much of the repeated control flow. Running `opt -O3 -S` followed by `llc -O3 -mcpu=apple-m5` reduces this to 37 encoded instructions (the symbol spans 148 bytes, or 37 AArch64 instructions) across four explicit branch-target labels (five blocks including entry) and removes stack accesses; default-CPU `llc -O3` emits 38 from the same optimized IR. The source repeats the same `start` normalization and `end` clamp in result-length contracts, pointer-offset contracts, and internal assertions; at O0, those expressions become separate load/phi/branch chains, while InstCombine and CFG simplification fold much of that work. This points to contract-expression sharing or a narrowly scoped IR canonicalization pass as a code-shape opportunity, but the fixture is deliberately contract-heavy and is not a compile-time hotspot profile or the runtime's short `string_view_slice` implementation. These pass counts establish emitted shape only; compilation time, runtime behavior, and source-level parity were not measured.

## Follow-up: exact SMT proof-cache boundaries

Static review of the current Core proof-cache WIP found one open performance follow-up and one
correctness issue that has since been fixed in source. First, a new
Analyzer's first `smtCachedQuery` miss initializes cache identity by hashing the compiler and solver
executables and running `z3 --version` behind a two-second guard. A retained compile-session creates
a new Analyzer for each request, so warm disk hits may still pay this process and file-I/O cost per
request even when the solver process itself is not spawned. Existing no-spawn tests prepopulate the
in-memory cache and observe the long-lived solver `SpawnTime`; they do not measure the `--version`
probe. Add an identity-preparation counter and compare cold versus warm disk hits before deciding
whether compiler identity can be cached per process and solver identity refreshed on executable
replacement.

The raw requires-vacuity SAT path previously cached `{proven:false}` without the
model/counterexample payload, allowing a raw query for `requires not P` to collide with a later
negated-obligation VC query for `P` and suppress its counterexample on a cache hit. The latest Core
diff fixes this by not caching raw SAT declines; raw UNSAT results remain cacheable. It also adds
`TestSMTRawSatDoesNotHideRegularCounterexample` for the collision. This is source-review evidence
only: the regression has not yet been run here, so diagnostic parity is not yet qualified. The
per-Analyzer identity-preparation cost above remains open and should be measured separately.

There is also an unresolved trust boundary for the disk cache. `contains` accepts an `unsat` record
when its checksum equals `SHA256(schema || key || "unsat")`; this is a public integrity checksum,
not proof evidence or authentication. A writer able to place a record for a known query key can
compute the checksum and make an unproved obligation look cached as UNSAT. `MkdirAll(..., 0700)`
only sets permissions on newly created directories; reads do not verify the configured cache root's
owner, permissions, or path components. Since this cache can bypass a solver proof, do not enable it
in a trust domain where another principal can write the cache. Before broadening use, either enforce
and document a verified owner-private directory boundary, or persist evidence that can actually be
validated as a proof. A MAC is useful only if its key is protected from cache writers. This is a
source-review finding and has not been exercised as an exploit or fixed.

## Work to prioritize

1. Capture a fresh profile from a compiler product that matches the current tree. Record semantic, IR-generation, object-emission, and edit-to-executable wall time separately. The existing stage1 product and LLVM dump do not qualify.
2. Keep semantic reuse opt-in until an edit beats a fresh compile end to end. Replace repeated reflective AST serialization with parser-owned token/body spans or stable syntax-node identities for source-origin functions. Then replace whole-declaration-surface admission for every body with exact per-body dependencies: referenced names and call targets, including negative and ambiguous lookups, plus visibility, global mutability, effects, and options. `NameLookup` already demonstrates a finer exact-projection query. Keep whole-surface invalidation as the fallback when dependency extraction is incomplete, and retain cold fallback for synthetic or unsupported trees.
3. Requalify the native-partition cache against the current Core source, preserving the same per-stage and whole-pipeline measurements. The current codegen implementation still performs whole-module optimization, partition cloning, and bitcode serialization before looking up completed objects, so a cache hit only avoids final target emission. To skip those costs, restructure the pipeline around stable, dependency-tracked codegen units and cache at a stage whose green result can reuse the corresponding prior bitcode/object product. Merely caching pre-O3 IR while still running whole-module O3 would not remove the measured optimizer cost. Keep pre-LTO and post-LTO products separate; identities must include target and ABI, optimization and LTO/inlining context, compiler and LLVM versions, stable partition ownership, and every dependency that can affect generated code. Cross-unit optimization must invalidate each affected product. Then expose the verified path through normal compilation; the selected-snapshot benchmark is promising but does not establish current-source behavior.

These boundaries match patterns in established compilers: [Rust's query runtime](https://github.com/rust-lang/rust/blob/main/compiler/rustc_query_impl/src/execution.rs#L2585) marks dependency nodes green and skips execution for unchanged query keys; [Zig's compilation state](https://github.com/ziglang/zig/blob/master/src/Compilation.zig#L2288) distinguishes whole-compilation cache state from long-lived incremental state; [Clang's AST writer](https://github.com/llvm/llvm-project/blob/main/clang/lib/Serialization/ASTWriter.cpp#L2601) tracks imported and undeclared-use module dependencies when deciding which module maps affect a serialized AST. For Elisa, this supports moving from whole-input reuse to per-body/query green-red reuse, then to codegen-unit reuse whose dependency graph includes transitive module and inline-body inputs.

The syntax-manifest prototype still materializes owner candidates on cold sessions, so manifest selection only helps warm reuse. Candidate preparation, reuse validation, and publication should consume one shared expression-visit budget; otherwise a failed manifest attempt can spend nearly its full budget and restart fallback with a fresh one. Stage manifest and syntax-lineage edits until commit so a fallback preserves prior session state. The reviewed pipeline comparison showed no speedup and about 3.6k extra allocations, so this structure needs a corrected cold-path design and matched measurement before promotion.
